    Glossary

    Terms that appear in several lessons or mean different things in different parts of the guide. Each entry links to the lessons that cover it.

    Attribution
    Assigning credit for conversions or revenue to channels or touchpoints. Rules such as first-touch and last-touch, Markov removal effects and survey responses describe how credit is divided. Estimating how many conversions a channel added requires a causal comparison such as a holdout. See Attribution Rules · Markov Attribution · Survey Attribution · Incrementality
    Average treatment effect (ATE, ATT, CATE)
    The average of Y(1) − Y(0), the difference between a unit’s potential outcomes with and without treatment. The ATE averages over the eligible population, the ATT over the units that received treatment, and the conditional average treatment effect (CATE) over units with a specified baseline profile. See Causal Graphs and Identification · Difference-in-Differences · Uplift Modeling
    Balance
    How similar treated and comparison groups are on measured baseline characteristics, often summarized by standardized mean differences (SMDs). After randomization, balance checks help diagnose implementation. After matching or weighting, they show how much measured difference remains. They say nothing about unmeasured characteristics. See Randomized Assignment and Blocking · Propensity Scores, Matching, and Weighting
    Baseline
    Has three meanings in the guide. In experiments and comparisons, a baseline is a measurement or characteristic recorded before treatment, such as prior spend. In a marketing mix model, baseline is the modeled demand under the model’s reference inputs, outside the media terms. In Markov attribution, the baseline probability is the chance of conversion with every channel present. See Randomized Assignment and Blocking · Difference-in-Differences · Marketing Mix Modeling · Markov Attribution
    Blocking
    Randomizing separately within groups of similar units, such as baseline activity bands, so each block is split between arms in the planned proportions. When allocation fractions differ across blocks, weight each within-block difference by the block’s share of enrollment. See Randomized Assignment and Blocking · Offer Experiment
    Break-even
    The smallest effect at which an action covers its cost. It is a business threshold, separate from statistical significance: an estimate can differ from zero and still fall short of break-even. With contribution margin m and media as the only incremental cost, the break-even incremental revenue ROAS is 1/m. See Offer Experiment · Sample Size & Power · Marketing Mix Modeling
    Calibration
    Adjusting one estimate with evidence from another source. Holdout calibration rescales platform-reported credit by the ratio of a past holdout’s incremental conversions to the conversions the platform reported during the test. Calibrating a marketing mix model uses a lift test as a prior or likelihood for a channel’s effect. Both assume the test evidence transfers to what is being adjusted. Survey calibration compares survey-implied ROAS with platform-reported ROAS. See Platform Credit Overlap & Holdout Calibration · Calibrating an MMM with a Lift Test · Survey Attribution
    Carryover (adstock)
    The part of an ad’s effect that continues after the week it runs. In an adstock transformation, the carryover rate λ is the fraction of exposure retained each week, and the half-life is the time for the remaining exposure from one spend pulse to fall by half. See Saturation & Carryover · Marketing Mix Modeling
    Churn and retention
    Churn is the share of customers who leave over a period, and retention is the share who stay. Compare cohorts at the same time since signup, with a common definition of churn and a known end date for the data. A churn risk score predicts who is likely to leave. It does not show whose retention an offer would change. See Churn · Retention · Retention Interventions
    Cohort
    A group defined by a shared starting event and followed over time. The guide uses signup cohorts for retention, adoption cohorts that start treatment on the same date in difference-in-differences, daily assignment cohorts in an A/B test, and a matched cohort of members and controls. See Retention · Difference-in-Differences · Analyzing A/B Tests · Propensity Scores, Matching, and Weighting
    Confidence interval
    A range from a procedure that, under its assumptions, covers the true value at a stated rate, such as 95%, in repeated samples. Its half-width, the margin of error, is a critical value times the standard error. A Bayesian credible interval instead assigns probability to the parameter given the priors and data. In market basket analysis, “confidence” means the attached rate, not an interval. See Reading Regressions · Analyzing A/B Tests · Market Basket Analysis
    Confounding
    A common cause of treatment and outcome that mixes the treatment effect with preexisting differences in a raw comparison. Random assignment breaks the link between treatment and confounders. Observational designs adjust for measured confounders and remain open to bias from unmeasured ones. See Causal Graphs and Identification · Randomized Assignment and Blocking · Propensity Scores, Matching, and Weighting
    Control group
    Units that do not receive the treatment, used to estimate what the treated units’ outcomes would have been without it. Random assignment makes the control group comparable by design. An observational comparison group, including matched controls, is comparable only under assumptions such as exchangeability or parallel trends. See Randomized Assignment and Blocking · Propensity Scores, Matching, and Weighting · Difference-in-Differences
    Cost per acquisition (CPA)
    Spend divided by conversions. The Budget Decision Engine compares current CPA with a goal. A cost per incremental conversion instead divides spend by the conversions a test estimates the spending added. See Budget Decision Engine · Calibrating an MMM with a Lift Test
    Critical value and degrees of freedom
    A critical value is the cutoff a test statistic must exceed to reject the null hypothesis at a chosen significance level. For t and F tests it depends on the degrees of freedom, which grow with sample size. As degrees of freedom grow, the t-distribution approaches the normal. See t-distribution · F-distribution · Sample Size & Power
    Customer lifetime value (CLV)
    The value a customer generates over the relationship. The guide estimates it from historical averages, or for subscriptions by discounting expected monthly gross profit under a churn probability. Under a contract, churn is observed. Without one, whether a customer is still active has to be inferred, as in BG/NBD. See Customer Lifetime Value · Contractual and Non-Contractual CLV
    Exchangeability
    Treated and comparison groups would have the same average outcomes under the same treatment. Random assignment makes groups exchangeable by design. Conditional exchangeability requires this only within levels of measured covariates, which means no unmeasured confounding given those covariates. See Causal Graphs and Identification · Uplift Modeling
    Geo test
    An experiment that assigns a spend change to markets rather than to individuals. In a paired geo test, markets are matched in pairs, one market in each pair is randomly assigned the change, and the effect is estimated from the paired differences. Markets must not affect each other’s outcomes. See Calibrating an MMM with a Lift Test · Offer Experiment
    Holdout
    A randomly selected group withheld from a campaign or treatment and used as the control. Comparing it with the exposed group estimates incremental outcomes. Holdout results can also calibrate platform-reported credit and marketing mix models. See Incrementality · Platform Credit Overlap & Holdout Calibration · Calibrating an MMM with a Lift Test
    Incrementality
    The change in an outcome caused by a campaign or treatment: outcomes with it minus the outcomes that would have occurred without it. Observed or attributed revenue includes sales that would have happened anyway, so estimating incrementality requires a causal comparison such as a randomized holdout. See Incrementality · Incremental Lift Report
    Intention-to-treat (ITT) effect
    The effect of assignment, comparing outcomes by assigned group whether or not units took up the treatment. Random assignment identifies it, given complete follow-up and no interference. The effect of actual receipt among compliers, a local average treatment effect (LATE), also requires relevance, no direct effect of assignment, and monotonicity. See Randomized Assignment and Blocking · Offer Experiment · Evaluate a Loyalty Program
    Key performance indicator (KPI)
    A measured quantity that tracks a business outcome, with a defined unit, denominator and follow-up window. A measurement plan connects each KPI to the events that record it. In a marketing mix model, the KPI is the outcome the model fits, such as revenue. See Measurement Plan · Plan Builder · Marketing Mix Modeling
    Lift
    In an experiment, the difference in an outcome between treatment and control, such as conversion-rate points. Relative lift divides that difference by the control rate. A lift test is an experiment that measures it. In market basket analysis, lift (affinity) divides the attached rate for a pair by the overall rate for the second item. A value of 1 means the items appear together as often as expected if they were independent. See Analyzing A/B Tests · Calibrating an MMM with a Lift Test · Market Basket Analysis
    Marketing mix model (MMM)
    A model of aggregate sales or another KPI as baseline demand plus modeled media response and other measured drivers such as prices and promotions. Different channel contributions can fit the same sales history. Interpreting a modeled response as the effect of a spend change requires a credible causal comparison, such as a lift test. See Marketing Mix Modeling · MMM Readiness · Calibrating an MMM with a Lift Test
    Mediator
    A variable on the path from treatment to outcome, such as search interest between advertising and sales. The total effect combines the direct path and the indirect path through the mediator. Adjusting for a mediator removes part of the total effect. See Causal Graphs and Identification · Path Analysis
    Minimum detectable effect (MDE)
    The smallest true effect a design would detect with the target power at the chosen significance level. It describes sensitivity against zero, which is different from clearing a break-even threshold. See Sample Size & Power · Offer Experiment
    Multiple comparisons
    Testing several arms, outcomes or subgroups raises the chance that at least one shows a false positive. Bonferroni and Holm corrections control the family-wise error rate. Benjamini–Hochberg controls the expected false-discovery proportion. Choosing among many analyses after seeing the results creates the same problem without a visible count. See P-Hacking and Replication · Sample Size & Power
    Overlap
    Units in the target population have a meaningful chance of appearing in either the treated or the comparison group. This is also called common support. Without overlap, a comparison relies on extrapolation, and matching can leave treated units unmatched. See Propensity Scores, Matching, and Weighting · Uplift Modeling
    P-value and statistical significance
    A p-value is the probability of a result at least as extreme as the one observed, assuming the null hypothesis is true. It is not the probability that the null hypothesis is true. A result is statistically significant when the p-value is at or below a chosen threshold α, often 0.05. A result that is not significant does not show the effect is zero. See P-Values · Reading Regressions · Analyzing A/B Tests
    Parallel trends
    The difference-in-differences assumption that treated and comparison groups would have had the same average change in outcome without treatment. Pre-treatment trends can reveal an implausible comparison, but they cannot confirm the assumption after treatment. See Difference-in-Differences
    Potential outcomes
    The outcomes a unit would have under each treatment condition, written Y(1) with treatment and Y(0) without. Only one is observed for each unit, so causal effects compare group averages under assumptions that make the missing outcomes estimable. See Causal Graphs and Identification · Uplift Modeling · Offer Experiment
    Power
    The probability that a test rejects the null hypothesis when a specified true effect exists, given the design, sample size and significance level. Set the effect size to the smallest difference that would change the decision. See Sample Size & Power
    Prior and posterior
    In a Bayesian analysis, the prior describes what is assumed about a parameter before seeing the data. The posterior combines the prior with the data through the likelihood, and a credible interval is a range of the posterior. In the marketing mix lessons, priors on channel effects can come from lift tests. See Marketing Mix Modeling · Calibrating an MMM with a Lift Test · Difference-in-Differences
    Propensity score
    The probability that a unit receives treatment given its observed pre-treatment covariates. It is used to check overlap and to match, stratify or weight comparison groups. It differs from a response model’s predicted probability of an outcome, such as churn risk, and from uplift, which estimates how treatment changes the outcome. See Propensity Scores, Matching, and Weighting · Estimate Effects
    Randomization
    Assigning treatment by a chance mechanism fixed before assignment, so that assignment is unrelated to the units’ potential outcomes. It supports estimating the effect of assignment among the enrolled units. Generalizing to a wider population requires random sampling or other assumptions. See Randomized Assignment and Blocking · Offer Experiment
    Response curve
    The modeled relationship between spend on a channel and its outcome. Average return divides the channel’s modeled contribution by its total spend. Marginal return is the slope at the current spend, the additional outcome from a small increase. See Marketing Mix Modeling · Saturation & Carryover
    Return on ad spend (ROAS)
    Revenue divided by ad spend. Its meaning depends on the revenue counted. Apparent or platform-reported ROAS uses observed or credited revenue, including sales that would have occurred anyway. Incremental ROAS uses the revenue a causal comparison attributes to the spend. Average ROAS divides by total spend, and marginal ROAS is the return on the next dollar. The MMM calibration lesson calls incremental revenue per dollar of spend ROI. See Incrementality · Marketing Mix Modeling · Survey Attribution · Budget Decision Engine
    Sample ratio mismatch (SRM)
    A difference between the recorded number of units in each arm and the planned allocation, checked with a goodness-of-fit test. An SRM is a reason to trace assignment, logging and exclusions before interpreting the results. See Randomized Assignment and Blocking · Analyzing A/B Tests
    Saturation
    Diminishing response at higher spend: beyond some point, each additional dollar adds less outcome. On an S-shaped curve, such as a Hill curve with exponent above 1, marginal return rises first, peaks and then declines. A marketing mix model can apply saturation before or after adstock. See Saturation & Carryover · Marketing Mix Modeling
    Selection bias
    Bias from how units enter the treated group or the observed data. Self-selection, such as frequent buyers joining a loyalty program, mixes the effect with preexisting differences. Selective observation, such as spending recorded only for customers who link receipts, can bias estimates even without a treatment comparison. See Evaluate a Loyalty Program · Heckman Selection Correction · Propensity Scores, Matching, and Weighting
    Spillover
    One unit’s treatment changes another unit’s outcome, such as advertising in one market reaching shoppers in a neighboring market. It is also called interference. Spillover can contaminate the control group. Designs either keep comparisons outside the exposure or measure exposure and estimate its effect. See Difference-in-Differences · Calibrating an MMM with a Lift Test · Randomized Assignment and Blocking
    Standard error
    The estimated standard deviation of an estimate across repeated samples, a measure of its precision. A t-statistic divides an estimate by its standard error. Standard errors must reflect the design, such as clustered assignment or reused matched controls. See Reading Regressions · Propensity Scores, Matching, and Weighting · Sample Size & Power
    Support
    Has two meanings in the guide. In market basket analysis, support is the share of baskets that contain a pair of items. In causal comparisons and models, support is where data exist: overlap means both groups appear at the covariate values of interest, and a model’s fit outside its training support relies on assumptions rather than data. See Market Basket Analysis · Propensity Scores, Matching, and Weighting · Marketing Mix Modeling
    Survival and hazard
    Survival is the share of a starting group still active at a given time. The hazard is the rate of leaving among those still active. With a constant hazard, the chance of staying another month does not depend on how long a customer has already stayed. See Contractual and Non-Contractual CLV · Churn · Difference-in-Differences
    Uplift
    The difference in an outcome, such as conversion probability, under treatment versus control for customers with a given baseline profile. It is a conditional average treatment effect. Targeting by uplift contacts profiles whose expected benefit exceeds the contact cost, which differs from targeting by churn or purchase risk. See Uplift Modeling · Retention Interventions · Incremental Lift Report
