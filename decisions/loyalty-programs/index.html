    Evaluate a Loyalty Program

    Estimate how spending would have changed for members had they stayed out of the loyalty program.

    Why member spending is a poor baseline

    Customers who already buy frequently may be more likely to join. If they would have spent more anyway, a post-enrollment comparison overstates lift. If members would have spent less than nonmembers without the program, the post-enrollment comparison understates lift. The direction of bias depends on who joins and how their spending would have changed without the program. To see this as a causal graph, open Causal Graphs and Identification and choose Unmeasured common cause. Its hidden U plays the role of a trait such as engagement that affects both joining and spending.

    Choose a comparison

    If you can randomize who receives an invitation, compare outcomes by invitation assignment. That estimates the effect of the invitation, including customers who decline. Encouragement and uptake shows how to estimate the effect of joining among customers who join only when invited, and the assumptions that estimate requires.

    If you have historical enrollment data, compare pre- and post-enrollment spending with a credible untreated group and check baseline overlap, pre-program trends, and concurrent changes. Match the cohort builds that group for a simulated loyalty cohort whose members spent more before joining. The Difference-in-Differences Lift Calculator then compares spending changes for those members and their matched controls.

    To evaluate profitability, compare incremental contribution margin over the same follow-up window with rewards, discounts, and operating costs.
