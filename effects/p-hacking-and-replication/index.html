    Why these results are not 96 independent experiments

    The same simulated people appear in many analyses, so the results are related. The four outcomes are different measures on a common index-point scale; choosing a different outcome or subgroup can change the question being answered. The confidence intervals and p-values are calculated one analysis at a time. They do not account for searching across 96 alternatives.

    Independent replication

    We generated an independent sample of 320 new simulated people and randomly assigned the same empty group labels. Again, no treatment exists, and both groups’ scores come from the same process. Your chosen outcome, subgroup, exclusions, and statistical model are applied unchanged.

    What this demonstrates: an effect picked because it looked impressive can overstate the true effect (sometimes called the “winner’s curse”). Replication estimates fluctuate around the true zero effect, but some can still pass p < .05 by chance. A nonsignificant replication is not proof that the effect is exactly zero.

    One experiment with three arms

    Each arm enrolls 1,000 accounts. The primary outcome is 30-day conversion. Control converts 200 accounts, Offer A converts 242, and Offer B converts 252. Both planned contrasts use the same control. Choose the family and see which two-sided pooled z tests reject at a family alpha of 5%.

    For k arms, there are k − 1 control contrasts or k(k − 1)/2 pairs. For m independent true nulls, the chance of at least one false positive at unadjusted alpha is 1 − (1 − α)m. Shared controls or metrics generally create dependence. Bonferroni and Holm control family-wise error under arbitrary dependence. Šidák’s exact calculation assumes independence. Benjamini–Hochberg controls the expected false-discovery proportion under its usual independence or positive-dependence conditions.

    Specify which primary contrasts and outcomes can drive the decision, label supporting metrics and exploratory subgroups, and lock the horizon and analysis before reading outcomes. If multiple metrics can independently trigger the same decision, include them in the family. Use prespecified sequential inference if outcome data can stop the test early. Carry the planned testing level into binary-outcome sizing.
