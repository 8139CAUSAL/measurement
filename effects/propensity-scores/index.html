Propensity Scores and Balance

How comparable are treated and untreated observations on measured characteristics?

Inputs
Treatment and baseline covariates. For weighting, include outcomes and an observation indicator.
Assumptions
Check overlap and assess balance on the observed covariates.
Prerequisites
Incrementality, Causal Graphs and Identification

Evaluating Causal Inference models. How to check for common support and covariate balance using Box Plots and Love Plots.

A propensity score is the estimated probability that a unit receives the treatment, given its observed covariates. In observational studies where randomization is not possible, propensity scores let us approximate a randomized experiment by balancing the distribution of confounders between the treated and control groups.

Once scores are estimated (usually via logistic regression or gradient-boosted trees), they enable several adjustment strategies: matching pairs similar treated and control units, stratification bins units into score quintiles, weighting uses inverse probability weights, and regression adjustment includes the score as a covariate.

Propensity Score
e(X) = P(T = 1 | X)
Interpretation: A propensity score of 0.7 means the model estimates a 70% chance that this unit would receive treatment, based on its covariates. Two units with similar scores are considered comparable regardless of their actual treatment assignment — which is the foundation for valid causal comparisons.

Common support (overlap) means every unit has a non-trivial chance of appearing in either the treated or control group. When the propensity-score distributions barely overlap, matching is unreliable because many treated units have no comparable controls.

A Love Plot shows the standardized mean difference (SMD) for each covariate before and after matching. The goal is to shrink every dot inside the ±0.1 threshold, indicating adequate balance.

Unadjusted Adjusted
Goal: After matching, every covariate's SMD should fall within the ±0.1 threshold (the dashed vertical lines). Covariates outside that band suggest residual imbalance that could bias causal estimates. The teal dots should cluster tightly around zero.

Weighting

In two synthetic baseline strata of 100 units each, treatment is more common in the high stratum. Reweight each arm toward the same target mix. For propensity e, the ATE weights are 1/e for treatment and 1/(1−e) for control. The ATT uses 1 for treatment and e/(1−e) for control.

Normalize within arms for the Hájek mean. Weight effective sample size (ESS) is (Σw)² / Σw² within each arm. Before and after standardized mean differences use the same pooled unweighted standard deviation. The conventional |SMD| < 0.1 guide describes balance on the measured covariate.

Check the existing overlap box plots before weighting and compare these weighted diagnostics with the matching Love plot. Similar estimated scores alone provide limited information about individual comparability. Clipping changes weights and can bias the target estimate. Trimming excludes units and changes the supported target. A missing treatment arm still requires a restriction or a different design.

Uncertainty after estimating the weights

Estimated propensities can improve precision in some designs. Standard errors that treat weights as fixed can be conservative or anticonservative. In a bootstrap, resample independent units or clusters and refit the full procedure. A regression that residualizes treatment adequately can implicitly weight heterogeneous effects by treatment variance. Variance of IPW estimators

Missing outcomes

Compare measured-baseline loss with loss driven by an unseen outcome. The cohort, treatment and conversion totals come from a simulation. The true effect is visible here for checking what each estimator recovers. Missing outcome values would be unknown in a real cohort.

Joint weighting and the augmented arm mean

Use treatment exchangeability and missing at random within treatment and baseline strata, with treatment and observation support and a well-defined outcome for all target units. Equal attrition rates are compatible with outcome-dependent selection.

For arm d, joint weights divide the treatment weight by the observed fraction πd in that arm and baseline stratum. For the ATE this is 1/(pdπd). The augmented ATE arm mean averages the working outcome regression μd(X) across everyone and adds the full-cohort average of observed arm residuals divided by pdπd. Other units contribute zero to that correction. For ATT, this example standardizes target predictions and corrections to the treated baseline mix.

Under identification, a correct outcome regression or a correct joint probability can give a consistent ATE estimate. Efficiency and inference also depend on estimation rates and other regularity conditions. These two-stratum estimates use empirical cell probabilities, so the untruncated joint weighting and augmentation agree algebraically. Bia, Huber and Lafférs on sample selection
